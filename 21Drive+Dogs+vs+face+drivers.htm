What Is a Drive Dog? What Is a Face Driver? Which One Should You Choose?

drive dogs (also called lathe dogs or carriers) were the standard method for turning workpieces between centers. Then came the face driver – a modern alternative that often eliminates the drive dog entirely.

Choose a drive dog for simple, low‑cost turning between centers, especially in small shops or for one‑off parts where speed is not critical. Choose a face driver when productivity, accuracy, and safety matter – production runs, hard turning, gear hobbing, and any application that demands complete machining in one setup.

A face driver is a precision workholding device mounted directly to the lathe spindle. Instead of gripping the OD, it engages the workpiece end face. The workpiece is pressed between the face driver and a tailstock center. Hardened drive pins – typically made of S7 tool steel or carbide – penetrate the end face under tailstock pressure, transmitting full torque directly. A spring‑loaded center point ensures exact alignment with the spindle axis.

The critical advantage of a face driver is one‑setup complete machining. Because no clamp or dog occupies the OD, the entire length of the workpiece can be turned in a single operation. This eliminates re‑chucking, cuts cycle times dramatically, and guarantees perfect concentricity between all diameters. Face drivers are also balanced and compact, allowing much higher spindle speeds. With no protruding tails, they are safer to use and easily integrated into automated loading systems. A drive dog clamps onto the outside diameter (OD) of a workpiece. It has a protruding tail – either straight or bent – that fits into a slot or against a drive pin mounted on a faceplate. As the spindle rotates, the faceplate pushes the dog’s tail, which then turns the workpiece. The opposite end of the part is supported by a tailstock center.

This design is simple, reliable, and widely used in traditional and home workshops. However, it has fundamental limitations. The clamp occupies a portion of the workpiece, meaning you cannot machine the entire length in one setup. After turning one section, you must reposition the dog or flip the part to finish the remaining OD. This second operation adds time and introduces concentricity errors. Furthermore, drive dogs are unbalanced. The protruding tail limits maximum spindle speed and creates a snagging hazard, especially on older lathes.
