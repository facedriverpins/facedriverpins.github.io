Drive pins and center pin work together in a face driver but serve very different purposes.

Drive Pins:Think of them as the engine. There are three to six of them, arranged symmetrically around the center pin. They are designed to actively bite into the material of the part's end face, providing the "traction" needed to transmit the spindle's torque for material removal.

Center Pin:Think of it as the steering wheel. It is the single, largest pin at the very axis of rotation. It fits into a pre-machined 60° center hole on the workpiece, establishing the precise single-axis reference point (datum) to ensure highly accurate concentricity during machining.

The process is highly coordinated: the workpiece is first loaded against the center pin, which aligns it. Then, as the tailstock advances, the drive pins are forced into the end face, creating the bite. The machining operation begins, and the first cut is made moving towards the face driver to further embed the pins for maximum torque transmission.

A key design factor is whether the center pin is fixed or movable. A fixed center pin is used when the highest run-out accuracy is required, often for grinding operations, delivering precision within 0.002 mm. A movable center pin can retract slightly. This allows the face driver to automatically compensate for minor variations in the workpiece's end face, ensuring all drive pins make full contact for maximum driving force.

The center pin ensures the part is perfectly centered and stable, while the drive pins provide the grip and rotational force for machining. No single type of pin is better than another; they are a symbiotic team, each performing an essential, distinct function.
